\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We expected the proof in this paper to keep its shape when the calculus grows. A new sort of member adds constructors to types and propositions, evidence rules for them, and cases to the translation. The normalizer gains cases and the canonical forms theorem gains cases, while preservation and progress only ever ask that theorem for a shape. Four extensions have since been built, each as a separate copy of the development: paths with singleton types, capture checking in two versions, and capture classifiers. Each copy changed the target, the normalizer, and the canonical forms theorem, and preservation and progress grew as well. Each carries the same theorems: canonical forms of closed evidence, preservation, progress, the erasure simulation, and the safety of its source by translation. None needed tight typing, a pushback measure, or a semantic model. The copies are separate developments, so this is evidence for the expectation and not a theorem about it. The target of this paper was itself revised four times while the translation was being proven: once to restrict morphisms to templates, once to make resolution alias-tolerant, once to add self-bounds so that intersections need no restriction, and once to let bounds mention the self so that recursive types need none. None of those revisions added a case to preservation, and the third added two impossible cases to an inversion lemma of progress.

Paths did not go the way we predicted. We expected the translation to let-bind every path prefix that a type mentions, so that a target block exists for it. In the development terms stay in monadic normal form and paths appear in types only. The binder of an object literal carries a forest of blocks, one node per stable field, an alias is a forwarding node, and path evidence types a path at the precise type of its node. The source passes the two acceptance tests of Giarrusso et al.\ \cite{giarrusso2020scala}. That development branched before the restriction on recursive types was lifted here, and it still has it.

Recursive subtyping is the main feature of DOT that this paper leaves out. The calculus of Rompf and Amin \cite{rompf2016soundness} compares two recursive types under an assumption about the self, and \FCdot has no rule that checks a coercion under such an assumption. The composition templates of Section~\ref{sec:target} cover one instance. A coercion between two recursive types that chains two facts the source object asserts about its own members is typed, normalizes, and is checked, and its source derivation is checked in the published rules. A translation of recursive subtyping in general would have to extract a finite template from every derivation under a self assumption, and we have not proven that it can. A separate development gives that calculus a target of its own, with a coercion checked under the self assumption, and proves it safe. It is outside this paper.

A small surface language with a derivation-producing type checker now exists as well. Programs are written in the notation of this paper, an elaborator fills omitted lambda domains and self types, and a typer modeled on the subtype checker of the Scala 3 compiler returns the source derivation, which the pipeline translates, checks with the checker of \FCdot, and runs. The typer is sound because it returns derivations, and complete up to a recursion limit with respect to its algorithmic judgment. As Section~\ref{sec:related} notes, it does not have to decide subtyping. The use we expect to matter most remains open. A compiler for a language in the DOT family could emit evidence for the subsumption steps it takes, in the way GHC emits FC coercions, and have them validated independently of its own subtyping algorithm. That matters most for the parts of a compiler that have no reference semantics beyond a paper, and capture checking is one of them.
